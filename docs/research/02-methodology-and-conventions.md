# Methodology and conventions research notes

## 1. Classify the decision before modeling

The skill must distinguish:

- descriptive attribution of an existing/current system;
- internal design or procurement comparison;
- public comparative assertion;
- product carbon footprint;
- EPD/PEF/PCR-constrained study;
- causal policy/investment consequence;
- future/emerging technology assessment;
- organizational or social life-cycle assessment.

This classification controls functional equivalence, data quality, modeling approach, review, and claim strength.

## 2. Goal and scope

Capture commissioner, practitioner, decision, audience, intended application, reasons, external disclosure, comparative assertion, confidentiality, limitations, review plan, geography, reference time, technology, study type, impact coverage, reporting form, and update policy.

The skill should challenge objectives such as “prove product A is green.” Reframe them into answerable, neutral questions.

## 3. Functional unit and reference flow

A strong functional unit specifies quantified function, performance/quality, duration/lifetime, use conditions, geography/context, and reliability where material. Reference flows then quantify each alternative required to deliver that function.

Examples of missing dimensions:

- packaging: protection, volume, shelf-life loss, number of cycles;
- batteries: usable energy throughput, efficiency, duty, degradation, climate;
- buildings: floor-area service, occupancy, performance, reference study period;
- chemicals: purity/concentration and delivered performance;
- transport: passenger- or tonne-km with occupancy/load and route;
- equipment: throughput, uptime, lifetime, precision;
- food: edible/nutritional function and loss, when relevant.

A declared unit is not automatically a comparative functional unit.

## 4. System boundary

Describe lifecycle stages, foreground/background, included processes, infrastructure/capital goods, geographic/temporal/technology coverage, cutoffs, environmental compartments, and exclusions. Use both a diagram and a process list.

Common boundary labels—gate-to-gate, cradle-to-gate, cradle-to-grave, well-to-tank, tank-to-wheel, well-to-wheel—are shorthand, not complete definitions.

## 5. Cutoff and completeness

A cutoff policy must define criterion (mass, energy, environmental relevance, data availability, program rule), threshold, treatment of hazardous/high-impact minor flows, and cumulative completeness check. Never infer insignificance from low mass alone. Excluded processes must be tested or bounded when they could change conclusions.

## 6. Attributional versus consequential

Attributional LCA describes shares of an existing or scenario system using average/attributional relationships. Consequential LCA estimates changes caused by a decision using counterfactuals, market response, marginal supply, substitution, and constraints.

Do not:

- call an average-grid study consequential;
- add a generic avoided-product credit to make an attributional model causal;
- infer a marginal supplier solely from high impact;
- combine attributional foreground allocation with consequential background credits without explanation.

When the decision question does not require causal market effects, attributional modeling is often easier to audit. When causality matters, state the counterfactual and causal chain.

## 7. Multifunctionality

Use a deliberate hierarchy:

1. avoid multifunctionality by subdivision or redefining the unit process where feasible;
2. expand the system or use substitution when consistent with the approach and decision;
3. partition using a relevant physical/causal relationship;
4. use economic or another justified relationship when physical causality is not suitable.

For every allocation factor record formula, co-product quantities/quality, prices and year/currency if economic, sum-to-one check, sensitivity, and program/database interaction. Do not allocate a downstream process twice because both the foreground and database have already resolved multifunctionality.

## 8. Recycling and end of life

The skill must recognize and correctly implement:

- recycled-content/cut-off approach;
- end-of-life/avoided-burden approach;
- closed-loop approximation;
- shared/50:50 approaches;
- PEF circular footprint formula;
- PCR-specific module treatment;
- database system-model treatment such as ecoinvent cut-off, APOS, consequential, or EN15804.

Track collection, sorting, processing yield, quality loss, substitution ratio, market demand, recycled content, disposal, energy recovery, and credits. Report gross burdens and credits separately. Prevent double claims between primary producer, recycler, and secondary user.

## 9. Electricity and energy

Select electricity accounting based on the question and rule context:

- location-based/current average;
- supplier-specific/contractual under an applicable accounting rule;
- residual mix;
- marginal/consequential;
- time-matched hourly/subannual mix;
- future scenario mix.

Record voltage, geography, data year, imports, losses, onsite generation, storage losses, certificates, curtailment, and avoided generation assumptions. Do not claim physical additionality from a contractual instrument unless the selected framework supports that interpretation.

Keep HHV/LHV, gross/net power, and primary/final energy bases explicit.

## 10. Carbon conventions

Separate fossil, biogenic, land-use-change, uptake, storage, delayed release, removals, avoided emissions, and offsets. Record GWP source, assessment report, time horizon, feedback treatment where relevant, and characterization implementation.

Guardrails:

- biomass is not automatically carbon neutral;
- captured fossil carbon is not a removal from the atmosphere;
- temporary storage is not permanent removal;
- an offset does not normally reduce the physical product inventory;
- avoided emissions are a comparison against a counterfactual, not a negative inventory flow without method rules;
- double counting across actors and product systems must be checked.

## 11. Water, land, toxicity, and biodiversity

Water inventory must preserve source, basin/region, withdrawal, consumption, return, timing, and quality. Apply scarcity/deprivation only with compatible geography.

Land occupation and transformation need area, duration, prior/current use, location, and restoration; biodiversity methods remain context-sensitive and should be interpreted cautiously.

Toxicity categories are sensitive to substance identity, compartment, speciation, fate/exposure models, characterization coverage, and large factor uncertainty. Never equate potential toxicity indicators with site-specific risk or regulatory safety.

## 12. Data quality

Assess technological, geographical, and temporal representativeness; completeness; precision/uncertainty; methodological consistency; source reliability; and review. Use a pedigree approach where appropriate, but preserve raw metadata rather than reducing all quality to one score.

## 13. LCIA

Classification and characterization are mandatory for a standard LCIA profile; normalization, grouping, and weighting are optional/value-laden unless a program requires them. Avoid mixing factors from different methods without a mapping and consistency protocol. Audit unmapped elementary flows and unit/compartment mismatches.

## 14. Interpretation

Perform contribution/hotspot analysis, completeness, consistency, sensitivity, scenarios, uncertainty, benchmark triangulation, and dominance checks. A robust conclusion states the conditions under which it holds. A ranking reversal is a decision result, not an inconvenience to hide.

## 15. Review and claims

The intended audience determines review depth. Public comparative claims require heightened equivalence, transparency, uncertainty, and independent review. The skill prepares evidence and tracks findings but does not impersonate the reviewer or program operator.
